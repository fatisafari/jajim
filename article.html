The slow return of the cassette tape hiss

And the kids who never let it die.

By Sara Mehrabi · May 2026 · 12 min read

It starts, almost always, with a friend. Someone passes you a small plastic rectangle with handwriting on the spine, and for a second you do not know what to do with your hands. The case opens with that specific click, the kind of click that is not trying to be anything other than what it is. Inside, a tape. Inside the tape, ninety minutes of someone's care.

The cassette was supposed to be over. We were told this firmly, in the late nineties, and again in the early aughts, and then once more, with feeling, when streaming arrived to make the whole question of owning music feel quaint. The format was inefficient. It degraded. It hissed. You had to flip it.

The flip side

And yet — walk into any record shop in any small city this spring, and you will find a wooden crate near the counter with tapes in it. Independent labels are pressing runs of two hundred. Teenagers who were born after the iPod are buying portable players with foam-covered headphones and complaining, with genuine affection, about the sound.

"It's the only music I own that I cannot scroll past."

That line was offered by a seventeen-year-old in a basement, somewhere I had no business being on a Tuesday night, and I have been thinking about it ever since. Not because it explained the cassette revival — explanations are easy and usually wrong — but because it explained something about the people doing the reviving.

They are not nostalgic. They cannot be. They were not there. What they are, instead, is tired. Tired of infinite choice. Tired of the algorithm. Tired of the way a song ends and another song you did not ask for begins, smoothly, professionally, as if the silence between two pieces of music had no value of its own.

The hiss

A cassette is a vote against frictionless living. To play it, you must commit. To skip a song, you must guess. To make one for a friend, you must sit in a room with the music for as long as the music takes. Ninety minutes is ninety minutes. You cannot cheat.

There is something almost moral about this, though no one in the basement would put it that way. They would shrug, and pass you another tape, and tell you the third song is the good one. And you would believe them, because they had taken the time to know.
